## Introduction to Day 8

Welcome back to Understanding Buddhism Through Its Scriptures. Today is Day 8 of our module and it is our last class. Today, we will look at how Buddhist scriptures have changed in modern times. In taking up this theme, we are continuing what we did in Classes 6 and 7. We are looking at how different kinds of contexts shape and constrain how Buddhist scriptures are lived in the world.

We will return today, in different ways, to the notion of canon. We saw on Day 2 that in contrast to the Bible or to the Qur'an, Buddhist scriptures were often brought together in a canon that was understood to be open. This meant that new texts could be added to the canon.

Curiously, in modern times, it is not only the case that texts are added to the canons of Buddhist scriptures, but also that some texts drop out of the canon too. They drop out because they do not fit into what we expect of the world today because of what we have learned from science or because of what we have learned from historicism. They drop out because they do not seem to help us with what we take to be the pressing concerns of our day.

That texts have dropped out of the canons of Buddhist scriptures has happened throughout Buddhist history.

Modern scholars have found a few buried deposits of Buddhist scriptures and surprisingly, some of the texts that clearly were very important at the time that the texts were buried, judging by the number of manuscripts that there are of them, were not used or even known by Buddhists in later times. One example of this is the cache of manuscripts found at Gilgit, in present day Pakistan, which includes the Lotus Sutra, which we looked at in this module, as well as the Sanghata Sutra, a scripture than is barely-known in the Buddhist world today. [If you would like to know more about Gilgit, you can read [The Gilgit Manuscripts, the Oldest Manuscript Collection Surviving in Pakistan and India](http://www.historyofinformation.com/expanded.php?id=4678). If you would like to know more about the Sanghata Sutra, you can explore [sanghatasutra.netlify.com](https://sanghatasutra.netlify.com/).]

In the modern times, the process of scriptures dropping out of the canons has been far more pronounced than the addition of any new texts to the canon.

Modern canons of Buddhist scriptures have become smaller than were the canons of Buddhist scriptures in earlier times.

Of course, some of the scriptures that were important in the past continue to be prominent in modern times. This is the case with some scriptures in the Pali canon and with the Lotus Sutra.

Some scriptures that seem magical or purely mythological may receive little or no attention from Buddhists today.

Other scriptures that are judged by modern historians to have been added to Buddhist canons very late have been dropped from the functioning canons. They are studied not as *Dharma* but only as part of the history of Buddhism.

This is the case with many of the "apocryphal" Buddhist scriptures that were written by Chinese Buddhists in China. [If you would like to learn more about these scriptures, you can read [East Asian Apocryphal Scriptures: Their Origin and Role in the Development of Sinitic Buddhism](http://www.acmuller.net/articles/1998-03-apocrypha.html).]

Other scriptures receive more attention today than before because they seem to be more compatible with contemporary conceptions of human beings. Scriptures that seem to endorse the importance of human rationality and the independence of individual thought, such as the "Advice to the Kalamas," are more visible today, and in fact form something of a new canon today.

Some scriptures become prominent in contemporary canons for the resources they provide for pressing social needs of today. Those scriptures that provide resources for social change or promote collective well-being--in the form of care for the sick, for example—may be more used today than they were in the past.

We have also seen in this course how Buddhists incorporated into their inheritance practices and texts that originated outside of the Buddhist traditions.

In recent times, however, this process has in some ways been reversed. Today, the ideas and practices found in Buddhist scriptures are taught outside of Buddhism all together. One example of this is the widespread practice of "mindfulness" meditation practices in all sorts of secular institutions, from hospitals and prisons to schools and businesses.

It sometimes seems that for some modern Buddhists, the teachings found in Buddhist scriptures are addressed not to Buddhists as such, but to humanity at large. For example, the Dalai Lama, in speaking about the necessity of a collaboration between Buddhism and science, [has said](http://www.dalailama.com/messages/buddhism/science-and-religion) that the interest in promoting personal and social well-being is not a concern of "Buddhism as such or any other religious tradition - it is simply a matter of trying to make clear the potential of the human mind. Everybody, whether rich or poor, educated or uneducated, has the potential to lead a peaceful, meaningful life. We must explore as far as we can how that can be brought about."

## Reading: The Mangala Sutta

In the past, the Mangala Sutta was considered to be one of the most important scriptures in the Pali canon. Buddhist readers saw enormous significance in its contents and even though it is a relatively brief text, one commentary on it, which was written in northern Thailand in the fifteenth century, is over five hundred pages long.

When you read the Mangala Sutta below, ask yourself whether any one person could have all the virtues of living that it names. Or is the Mangala Sutta describing the virtues of a Buddhist community, rather than the virtues of an individual?

The idea of *mangala* is an important one in much of the Buddhist world but also in other Indian religions like Jainism and Hinduism. *Mangala* can be translated as a "blessing," or as a "good omen," or as "something auspicious." The definition of a sacrament that some Christians use--"a visible sign of an invisible grace"-would also work well for *mangala*.

If you want to explore a modern Buddhist commentary on the Mangala Sutta, one can be found [here](http://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/soni/wheel254.html).

## Reading: Advice to the Kalamas

"The Advice to the Kalamas" is a text from the Pali canon and it has become one of the most important Buddhist scriptures in the modern world. It often is taken as praise of the value of free inquiry in the religious life. Much has been claimed on the basis of this text but some of these claims have been called into question by some Buddhists.


Bhikkhu Bodhi, for example, says:
The discourse has been described as "the Buddha's Charter of Free Inquiry," and though the discourse certainly does counter the decrees of dogmatism and blind faith with a vigorous call for free investigation, it is problematic whether the sutta can support all the positions that have been ascribed to it. On the basis of a single passage, quoted out of context, the Buddha has been made out to be a pragmatic empiricist who dismisses all doctrine and faith, and whose Dhamma is simply a freethinker's kit to truth which invites each one to accept and reject whatever he likes.


But does the Kalama Sutta really justify such views? Or do we meet in these claims just another set of variations on that egregious old tendency to interpret the Dhamma according to whatever notions are congenial to oneself - or to those to whom one is preaching?

## Reading: Buddhism and Historicism

Day 4 in the module on Christianity in this course on "World Religions Through Their Scriptures" introduced the perspective that historicism brought to the study of the Bible. You can read below how this topic was introduced in the module on Christianity.

The same questions that were applied to the Bible through historicism have come to be applied to Buddhist scriptures by both scholars and by some Buddhists. It is possible then to read the same passage from the Christianity module here, and simply substitute "Buddhist scriptures" whenever "Bible" or "Gospel" occurs. By doing so, we remind ourselves that this way of engaging Buddhist scriptures comes from interpretive practices that were first developed with respect to the Christian Bible.

We saw an example of a Buddhist monk embracing the lessons of historicism in the letter by Bhikkhu Analayo on women's ordination that we looked at on Day 6.

The problems facing any attempt to approach Buddhist scriptures with a historical critical approach, however, are perhaps larger than those who used historical criticism for engage the Bible simply because of the sheer number of Buddhist scriptures. The manuscript evidence for early Buddhism is also very distant from the time of the beginnings of Buddhism, at least a thousand years.

Historical Criticism has changed how Buddhist Scriptures are approached, especially with the result that somehow the scriptures found in the Pali canon are more important because they are closer to the historical Buddha.

We will see in some other readings today that for some Buddhists, "historical-critical studies opened up the scriptures to the modern world."

**Historical Criticism [From the Christianity Module]**

In the wake of the Enlightenment and the rise of historicism, certain scholars and theologians began to interpret the Bible as an historical document in line with assumptions of science and historicism . Scholars taking a critical-historical approach began by asking basic historical questions like: Who wrote the books contained in the Bible? When and where were they written? What oral or literary sources were used and how were they edited to give distinctive theological teachings? Historians were concerned, too, with how the oral settings of preaching, mission, baptizing, teaching, and worship effected the selection of what tradition was passed on and what forms it would take.

A foundational task was to restore the original text, given that none of the original manuscripts written by the Biblical authors survive, and there are over 6000 handwritten copies of the writings contained in the New Testament alone, with hundreds of different variant readings. More recently, text critics have looked to the manuscript variants to investigate how the scriptural tradition was formed. For example, on Day 2, we saw that manuscripts offer several alternative endings of the Gospel of Mark. One might ask not only, how did the Gospel of Mark originally end, but also why, when, and by whom were these other endings written—and how do they affect the meaning of the Gospel? How were the Biblical texts received and interpreted by later followers—up to contemporary times?

Another major effort lay in understanding more fully the political, economic, cultural, and social aspects of the ancient Roman Mediterranean world where Christianity was born. This focus led to a burgeoning body of Biblical scholarship on wide array of topics concerning the archaeological record, history, religions, and cultures of the ancient Mediterranean world. Scholars using historical methods are exploring issues such as ancient sacrificial practices, trade routes used by Christian missionaries, letter-writing, and burial practices.

Underlying these investigations was (usually) the conviction that the true meaning of the Bible was to be found by impartial, objective investigation of the original texts in their ancient languages and original social-historical-religious contexts. These investigations were often inflected by rationalism, which eschewed any supernatural activity, especially miracles and prophecy. They mapped the events described in the Bible, as well as the writing of the Biblical texts themselves, chronologically on a linear time-line. Because they assumed that each ancient author wrote with particular purposes in mind and directed their works at specific audiences, it followed that harmonizing texts or using one part of the Bible to interpret another could only lead to distortion of the original meaning, thus discrediting long-standing practices that interpreted Christian scriptures as a unitary whole. With the ban on harmonization, theological diversity and factual discrepancies among the canonical texts became more apparent.

For some, historical-critical studies opened up the scriptures to the modern world and freed interpretation from what were perceived as the shackles of dogma and institutional rigidity. The social gospel movement, for example, spurred a new attention to establishing the kingdom of God on earth by applying Christian social ethics to problems such as poverty, crime, alcoholism, racial tensions, and child labor. For others, they challenged basic and dearly held convictions about the basis for the authority of scripture and the truth of teachings as fundamental as the virgin birth and resurrection of Jesus. 

## Reading: The Historical Buddha

One important way that historicism has changed the Buddhist canon is by the introduction of the idea of the historical Buddha.

You can read a representative account of the Buddha as a historical figure [here](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/art-asia/beginners-guide-asian-culture/buddhist-art-culture/a/the-historical-buddha).

When we begin with the Buddha as a historical figure, we are disposed to see the internal diversity of the Buddhist world as a product of the contingencies of history.

We want to remember that before historicism, many Buddhists saw the internal diversity of Buddhist scriptures as purposeful, a sign of how the Buddha taught skillfully, and not as accidents of history.

## Reading: The Dalai Lama on the Historical Buddha

**Preferring the Buddha as a Historical Figure**

In our readings on Day 7, we read something from *The Good Hear*t by the Dalai Lama. It was a passage in which the Dalai Lama explained what he saw in the account in the Gospel of Mark about Jesus teaching in parables.

Here is an excerpt from the same book. In it you will see the Dalai Lama acknowledging that there are different ways to see the Buddha and then expressing what his own preference is.

As you read the excerpt, try to note the places where the insights of historicism are assumed.

**From *The Good Heart***

Given that there is such diversity even among the Buddhist traditions, we should not have the impression that there is one homogeneous tradition, one definitive path as it were. Personally, I prefer to relate to Buddha as a historical figure and personality--someone who has perfected human nature and evolved into a fully enlightened being. However, according to certain schools of thought in Buddhism, Buddha is not just seen as a historical figure, but also shares a timeless, infinite dimension. In this context, although Buddha is a historical figure, the historicity of Buddha Shakyamuni would be seen as a skillful display of Buddha's compassionate action manifesting from the perfect timeless state of the *dharmakaya*, or Truth Body. Buddha Shakyamuni as a historical figure is known as the *nirmanakaya*, which means Emanation Body: an emanation that is assumed in order to suit the mental dispositions and needs of a particular time, place, and context. That emanation comes from a preceding emanation, the *sambhogakaya*, or perfect resourceful state, which has arisen from the timeless expanse of the dharmakaya. However, if we go into all these specifics now, we would have plenty of material for headaches and confusion!

The simplest way to regard Buddha Shakyamuni as a historical figure is as follows. For Buddhists--especially for those who are following a monastic way of life--Buddha was the founder of the Buddhist monastic tradition. He is the origin of the lineage of Buddhist monasticism. Fully ordained monks and nuns within that lineage must maintain complete acceptance of their vows of ordination. In order for someone to be a bhiksu, a fully ordained monk, or a bhiksuni, a fully ordained nun, he or she must be a human being. So if you relate to the Buddha as a fully ordained monk then that means you relate to him as a historical, human being.

[from The Dalai Lama, *The Good Heart: A Buddhist Perspective on the Teachings of Jesus* (Boston: Wisdom Publications, 1996), page 61]

## Reading: "Our Faith in Science" by the Dalai Lama

Just as the rise of historicism has changed how Buddhist scriptures are engaged for many Buddhists, so too has modern science changed how Buddhist scriptures are engaged by many Buddhists. In the opinion piece by the Dalai Lama that is found [here](http://www.nytimes.com/2005/11/12/opinion/our-faith-in-science.html?_r=0), entitled "Our Faith in Science," you will see how he views the presence of science and Buddhism in our contemporary times.

Note in particular, this comment of the Dalai Lama's:

"If science proves some belief of Buddhism wrong, then Buddhism will have to change. In my view, science and Buddhism share a search for the truth and for understanding reality. By learning from science about aspects of reality where its understanding may be more advanced, I believe that Buddhism enriches its own worldview."

## Reading: Buddhism and Democracy

One way that Buddhist Scriptures have changed in the modern world is that some Buddhists today come to them with questions about what they contribute to modern concerns, such as the value of democracy and human rights.

Approaching Buddhist scriptures with such questions in mind means that new aspects of the scriptures may come to the fore or that the Scriptures are spoken about generally, as supporting a particular perspective, without any reference to a particular passage in the scriptures themselves.

You will find [here](http://www.dalailama.com/messages/buddhism/buddhism-and-democracy) an statement by the Dalai Lama on "Buddhism and Democracy" that is in this vein.

It might be the case, of course, that some Buddhists and some scholars and students of Buddhism, having the assumptions of historicism in mind, would be suspicious of such readings of Buddhist scriptures: how could Buddhist scriptures contribute anything to our understanding of "human rights" when the notion of human rights did not exist at the time that the scriptures were written? 

## Reading: Mindfulness Based Stress Reduction

Another major change for Buddhist Scriptures in our world is that, in some cases, the ideas and practices that entered the world in a Buddhist scripture now circulate in the world quite independently of anything Buddhist at all.

This is the case with the practice of "mindfulness." As a contemplative practice in Buddhism, mindfulness is associated with the "focused attention" taught by texts like the Satipatthana sutta, which we looked at on Day 5.

Today, derivations from such Buddhist ideas and practices are taught as something "mindfulness-based," for example in "Mindfulness Based Stress Reduction" (MBSR) that is now taught in many hospitals as part of care for the sick. Other mindfulness based practices are taught today in schools and in prisons as well as in a variety of other contexts.
The value of such practices is to be confirmed not by their association with the Buddhist heritages but in terms of the lessons of science.
Look at the presentation of mindfulness as a practice at these medical websites, [here](http://www.mindful.org/the-science-of-mindfulness/) and [here](http://www.webmd.com/balance/tc/mindfulness-based-stress-reduction-topic-overview).

Would you say that the presentation of mindfulness practice at these websites is "Buddhist"? If so, in what way?

## Reading: Smokey the Bear Sutra

You will find here a new "Buddhist Scripture" by the American Buddhist poet, Gary Snyder. It is tongue in cheek, of course, but it is also quite serious at the same time, with its sense of urgency of the human responsibility to care for our planet.

One could read it as a contemporary example of an "open canon" among Buddhists.

Here, we will read Snyder's as an example of how "historical-critical studies opened up the scriptures to the modern world." When inherited scriptures do not help us enough with the pressing concerns of today, then perhaps we have to supplement them with other "scriptures," such as the "Smokey the Bear Sutra."

As you read this sutra, try to note those elements in it that you have already seen in this module about Buddhist Scriptures. For example, how it begins with something about the long lifespan of the Buddha which we saw in the Sutra of Golden Light and in the Lotus Sutra.

**SMOKEY THE BEAR SUTRA**
**BY GARY SNYDER**

Once in the Jurassic about 150 million years ago, the Great Sun Buddha in this corner of the Infinite Void gave a discourse to all the assembled elements and energies: to the standing beings, the walking beings, the flying beings, and the sitting beings--even the grasses, to the number of thirteen billion, each one born from a seed, assembled there: a Discourse concerning Enlightenment on the planet Earth.

"In some future time, there will be a continent called America. It will have great centers of power called such as Pyramid Lake, Walden Pond, Mt. Rainier, Big Sur, Everglades, and so forth; and powerful nerves and channels such as Columbia River, Mississippi River, and Grand Canyon. The human race in that era will get into troubles all over its head, and practically wreck everything in spite of its own strong intelligent Buddha-nature."

"The twisting strata of the great mountains and the pulsings of volcanoes are my love burning deep in the earth. My obstinate compassion is schist and basalt and granite, to be mountains, to bring down the rain. In that future American Era I shall enter a new form; to cure the world of loveless knowledge that seeks with blind hunger: and mindless rage eating food that will not fill it."

And he showed himself in his true form of
**SMOKEY THE BEAR**

A handsome smokey-colored brown bear standing on his hind legs, showing that he is aroused and watchful.

Bearing in his right paw the Shovel that digs to the truth beneath appearances; cuts the roots of useless attachments, and flings damp sand on the fires of greed and war;

His left paw in the mudra of Comradely Display--indicating that all creatures have the full right to live to their limits and that of deer, rabbits, chipmunks, snakes, dandelions, and lizards all grow in the realm of the Dharma;

Wearing the blue work overalls symbolic of slaves and laborers, the countless men oppressed by a civilization that claims to save but often destroys;

Wearing the broad-brimmed hat of the west, symbolic of the forces that guard the wilderness, which is the Natural State of the Dharma and the true path of man on Earth:

*all true paths lead through mountains--*

With a halo of smoke and flame behind, the forest fires of the kali-yuga, fires caused by the stupidity of those who think things can be gained and lost whereas in truth all is contained vast and free in the Blue Sky and Green Earth of One Mind;

Round-bellied to show his kind nature and that the great earth has food enough for everyone who loves her and trusts her;

Trampling underfoot wasteful freeways and needless suburbs, smashing the worms of capitalism and totalitarianism;

Indicating the task: his followers, becoming free of cars, houses, canned foods, universities, and shoes, master the Three Mysteries of their own Body, Speech, and Mind; and fearlessly chop down the rotten trees and prune out the sick limbs of this country America and then burn the leftover trash.

Wrathful but calm. Austere but Comic. Smokey the Bear will Illuminate those who would help him; but for those who would hinder or slander him...
HE WILL PUT THEM OUT.

Thus his great Mantra:

*Namah samanta vajranam chanda maharoshana Sphataya hum traka ham mam*

**"I DEDICATE MYSELF TO THE UNIVERSAL DIAMOND BE THIS RAGING FURY BE DESTROYED"**

And he will protect those who love the woods and rivers, Gods and animals, hobos and madmen, prisoners and sick people, musicians, playful women, and hopeful children:

And if anyone is threatened by advertising, air pollution, television, or the police, they should chant **SMOKEY THE BEAR'S WAR SPELL**:
**DROWN THEIR BUTTS**
**CRUSH THEIR BUTTS**
**DROWN THEIR BUTTS**
**CRUSH THEIR BUTTS**

And SMOKEY THE BEAR will surely appear to put the enemy out with his vajra-shovel.

Now those who recite this Sutra and then try to put it in practice will accumulate merit as countless as the sands of Arizona and Nevada.

Will help save the planet Earth from total oil slick.
Will enter the age of harmony of man and nature.
Will win the tender love and caresses of men, women, and beasts.
Will always have ripened blackberries to eat and a sunny spot under a pine tree to sit at.

AND IN THE END WILL WIN HIGHEST PERFECT ENLIGHTENMENT

We have come to the end of this course on Buddhism Through its Scriptures and I want to thank you for being part of it.
I hope that you have found this introduction to Buddhism Through Its Scriptures to be an encouragement to find more examples of Buddhist scriptures to read, not only to learn about Buddhism as a great religious movement but also to learn more about the human activity of scriptures and also to learn from Buddhist scriptures about yourself as a human being.

This has been a challenging course to teach and I am aware of its many shortcomings.
Buddhist folklore in Sri Lanka tells of a particular mythological bird, the cakavaka. Among the cakavaka's many virtues is its ability to drink only the milk when milk is mixed with water. In the past, Buddhist authors in Sri Lanka would sometimes ask their readers to be like cakavaka birds, to take what is true and useful—the milk—and to leave the mistakes and distortions—the water. I too ask you to be like the cakavaka bird, to take whatever you found to be useful in this class and otherwise to leave its shortcomings and failures.
Many of whatever successes there have been in this class—its milk—have come from your own contributions to discussions in the ideas, reactions, and other materials that you posted. Thank you for all of your contributions.
I would also like to thank all those who helped me with this course.
I am especially grateful to Alexis Bader for helping to think through the design of this course with me.
I thank Diane Moore for the idea of this course itself and her encouragement throughout.
I am grateful to Zachary Davis and Ryann Tagle for their essential contributions to putting this online course together, as well as their good cheer and patience.
I also thank my fellow instructors in the class: Alexis Bader, Charles Carstens, Kate Hartmann, Georgia Kashnig, Venerable Upali Sramon, and Devin Zuckerman. Their efforts have added to this class in many, many ways.
And many, many thanks to you all.
Let me close with the words of an ancient Buddhist wish:
"May you be happy!"
-Professor Hallisey --
