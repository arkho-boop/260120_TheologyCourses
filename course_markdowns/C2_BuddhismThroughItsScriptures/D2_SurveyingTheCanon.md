## Welcome to Day 2

Welcome to Day 2 of Buddhism Through Its Scriptures.

What you will look at today all falls under what we called "observables" in Day 1; if you need to refresh your memory on what this entails, look again at the section on "Two Poles of Buddhist Studies: Learning by Observing and Learning by Inferring" in Day 1.

In our materials today, you will find names and ideas that can help you to think about and then talk about Buddhist scriptures as they are found in the world. These names and ideas are tools that enable us to see things, help us to begin to understand them, and provide resources for both appreciating Buddhist scriptures and engaging them critically.

Some of the names and ideas come from the Buddhist heritages, others come from the academic study of religion. Some may seem familiar to you, others may seem new. All are invaluable and it is worth the effort to learn them well now. We will use them again and again throughout this course. We won't be able to talk together in our class without them; they will give us a shared conceptual vocabulary. You will be able to use them to express your own understanding to others in this class.

These names and the ideas that go with them do not only designate religious phenomena. They help us to organize what we see, they help us to make distinctions and they help us to make connections. They are essential tools that enable us to see the internal diversity of the Buddhist scriptures and also of the internal diversity of the Buddhist world itself. They provide ways for us to ask questions about what we see and they give us a way of sharing our answers to these questions with each other.

Like what they name, the tools themselves are diverse. A screwdriver is a tool for one task, a hammer is a tool for another. And like with these handtools, we will make a mess of things if we use our conceptual tools carelessly, as if they were interchangeable. Our tools are not interchangeable. To become skilled at using them, we want to keep them distinguished.

Our theme for the day is surveying the canon--or more accurately, the canons--that arrange Buddhist scriptures in patterns. What we will observe again and again is difference, difference between different canons and between different types of canons.

In other words, our theme today is canon, but what we will observe is difference and diversity.

Be sure to look at the video in today's resources on Buddhist voices. You will see in it other ways that difference and diversity present themselves in Buddhism.

## Where To Begin? Some Possible First Steps

In the picture above, you see a library of Buddhist scriptures. When you look at it, you can immediately see the first problem that we face: Where to begin? What should be the first book that we take off the shelves and where should we begin reading? Should we start at the 'beginning'? It is not clear just where that beginning might be.

This is not only a practical problem, it is also a conceptual and interpretive problem. Wherever we choose to begin sets in motion a way of seeing, one which inevitably will highlight some aspects of what we explore and push into the shadows, some others.

Let's consider some options, so we can see what is at stake.

One way of referring to Buddhist scriptures is to say that they are *Buddhavacana*, the word of the Buddha. It might seem then that where we should begin is with the life of the Buddha itself, and see how Buddhist scriptures somehow are expressions of how the Buddha lived and what he discovered. If we think of the Buddha as a historical figure, we can try to reconstruct his life and times not only from Buddhist sources, but also from what we know of the religious creativity and ferment that characterized the time in which he lived. Forms of Hinduism, associated with texts known as the Upanisads, and also the Indian religion known as Jainism are other religious movements that can be traced to the same historical context in which the historical Buddha lived.

Alternatively, we could begin with one of the biographies of the Buddha that are found in Buddhist scriptures themselves. One of these that is often highlighted, because of its autobiographical character, is the *Ariyapariyesana sutta*, "The Noble Search," in which the Buddha remembers the beginning of his own religious life in the following way:

"I, too, monks, before my Awakening, when I was an unawakened bodhisatta [a buddha-to-be], being subject myself to birth, sought what was likewise subject to birth. Being subject myself to aging... illness... death... sorrow... defilement, I sought [happiness in] what was likewise subject to illness... death... sorrow... defilement. The thought occurred to me, 'Why do I, being subject myself to birth, seek what is likewise subject to birth? Being subject myself to aging... illness... death... sorrow... defilement, why do I seek what is likewise subject to illness... death... sorrow... defilement? What if I, being subject myself to birth, seeing the drawbacks of birth, were to seek the unborn, unexcelled rest from the yoke: Unbinding [Nirvana]? What if I, being subject myself to aging... illness... death... sorrow... defilement, seeing the drawbacks of aging... illness... death... sorrow... defilement, were to seek the aging-less, illness-less, deathless, sorrow-less, unexcelled rest from the yoke: Unbinding?'

"So, at a later time, while still young, a black-haired young man endowed with the blessings of youth in the first stage of life — and while my parents, unwilling, were crying with tears streaming down their faces — I shaved off my hair & beard, put on the ochre robe and went forth from the home life into homelessness.

"Having thus gone forth in search of what might be skillful, seeking the unexcelled state of sublime peace, I went to Alara Kalama and, on arrival, said to him: 'Friend Kalama, I want to practice in this doctrine & discipline.'

[If you like, you can read the whole Ariyapariyesana sutta [here](http://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.026.than.html).]

To begin to explore Buddhist scriptures as emerging from the Buddha's life and times, from his own quest, is to see the Buddha's teaching as connected to some existential concerns. There is no doubt that this is a good place to start from. Remember that our emphasis on the Buddha's teaching as a refuge in Day 1 also was a way of highlighting how Buddhists have consistently commended coming to the Buddha's teaching from our own existential condition: we are beings who find ourselves in need of refuge, we are refugees when we come to the Buddha's teaching.

Another way of beginning is to start with the Buddha's First Sermon, a teaching that has been remembered and celebrated by Buddhists as when the Awakened One first set the "Wheel of Truth" in motion. In it, the Buddha teaches the Four Noble Truths, among other things:

"Now this, monks, is the noble truth of stress: Birth is stressful, aging is stressful, death is stressful; sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, & despair are stressful; association with the unbeloved is stressful, separation from the loved is stressful, not getting what is wanted is stressful. In short, the five clinging-aggregates are stressful.

"And this, monks, is the noble truth of the origination of stress: the craving that makes for further becoming — accompanied by passion & delight, relishing now here & now there — i.e., craving for sensual pleasure, craving for becoming, craving for non-becoming.

"And this, monks, is the noble truth of the cessation of stress: the remainderless fading & cessation, renunciation, relinquishment, release, & letting go of that very craving.

"And this, monks, is the noble truth of the way of practice leading to the cessation of stress: precisely this Noble Eightfold Path — right view, right resolve, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.

[If you like, you can read the whole Dhammacakkappavattanasutta [here](http://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn56/sn56.011.than.html).]

Beginning with the First Sermon is also a good place to start the study of Buddhist scriptures, and indeed this text does contain many distinctive Buddhist teachings, ones, like the Four Noble Truths, closely associated with Buddhism. We should be alert, however, to the possibility that to begin with the first sermon is to become inclined to see Buddhist scriptures in terms of their contents, and even more significantly, we should be alert to the possibility that to begin with the first sermon is to somehow privilege the teachings in it as more original or foundational than what is in other, 'later,' scriptures. We will see in our next class that many Buddhists have not seen things this ways. What is 'first' is not necessarily more original or more important, it could be just preliminary. To assume such a view, that the Buddha gave his most complete teaching later in his career, might incline one to see later scriptures as the Buddha's 'final' and thus most complete teachings and we shall see in the next class that this what some Buddhists have said.

We can also note that to begin with the First Sermon might encourage us to expect the contents of Buddhist scriptures to correlate with what with associate with the great divisions of the Buddhist heritages--such as Mahayana or Great Vehicle and the Lesser Vehicle or the Hinayana--or with the doctrinal positions of particular schools--such as the Theravada. We would quickly say that this version of the First Sermon belongs to the Theravada school of Buddhism now found in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia.

This course focuses on Buddhist scriptures and is not, strictly speaking a history of Buddhist thought. It is the case that the doctrinal positions of different Buddhist schools are always easily derived from the scriptures that these schools preserve. Still, it will be very useful for you to have just a basic working-knowledge of the broad categories Theravada and Mahayana, since most of the scriptures we will look at in this course are designated and then classified with these names. A useful overview of these divisions of Buddhist history can be found [here](http://www.buddhanet.net/e-learning/buddhistworld/schools1.htm). An illuminating comparison of their respective doctrinal positions and the geographical expanse can be found [here](http://www.buddhanet.net/e-learning/history/comparative.htm). As is always the case in such surveys, details often get simplified and even distorted in the act of generalizing, but the 'big picture' is still helpful to have.

Yet another place to begin is with the first words of many Buddhist scriptures: "thus I have heard" (*evam me sutam* in Pali). This little phrase is a marker that what is to follow is the word of the Buddha, taught by him and heard directly from him. Like when we read "Once upon a time," we know that a fairy tale is to follow, so when we hear or read "Thus I have heard," or some other variant translation like "Thus was heard by me" or "This is what I heard," we know that the some teaching of the Buddha is to follow.

Conventionally, these words are attributed to Ananda, the Buddha's personal attendant. As the Buddha's personal attendant, Ananda was always with the Buddha. He thus had the chance to hear whatever the Buddha taught over his long teaching career. After the Buddha died, five hundred enlightened monks were called together by Mahakasyapa in an assemby, so that they could hear together accounts of what the Buddha taught and then they could agree together that what was remembered was indeed genuine and could be trusted as "what the Buddha taught." Surrounded by the enlightened monks, Ananda recited the various sermons of the Buddha that he had heard, introducing each one with the phrase "Thus have I heard," and the other assembled monks agreed that what Ananda remembered and recited were indeed the teaching of the Buddha. Ananda recited the Buddha's instructions, his sermons or discourses addressed to particular people. Another monk, Upali, recited what he remembered the Buddha saying to monks on how they should live, and how they should regulate their lives together in a monastic community of men and women. The other assembled monks also agreed that what Upali remembered and recited were indeed the discipline, the *Vinaya*, of the Buddha.

This assembly is remembered as the First Great Council, and it's purpose was to set in motion a way of preserving and transmitting the word of the Buddha, such that the Buddha could continue to teach men and women after his life had ended. [If you like, you can read a fuller descriptive account of the First Council [here](http://www.buddhanet.net/e-learning/buddhism/lifebuddha/2_32lbud.htm); an example of a scriptural account of the First Council from the Vinaya in the Pali Canon can be found [here](http://www.sacred-texts.com/bud/sbe20/sbe20119.htm).] There were subsequent Councils after the first one; one of the most recent was held in Burma in the nineteen-fifties. [If you would like to see one list of these councils, a list which culminates in the Council held in Burma, see [here](http://www.tipitaka.org/chattha). There are other lists of Councils as well; the lists vary on what counts among later Councils, but the First Council is accepted by all.]

Accounts of the First Council are found in the literatures of many Buddhist schools. Beginning with an account of the First Council is also a good place to start the study of Buddhist scriptures, especially since these accounts introduce an idea of 'canon' as key to understanding the word of the Buddha. They also turn our attention to issues of transmission and the possibility that the word of the Buddha could change over time, whether by inattention to what it contains or the insertion of things that do not belong to what the Buddha taught. The accounts of the First Council also highlight the necessity of caring for Buddhist scriptures, to preserve them for the well-being of men and women in the future. The Councils represent great acts of caring for the Word of the Buddha, but every time a woman or a man copies a Buddhist text, they are also caring for the word of the Buddha, in a small way ensuring that it endures in the world.

## Reading: The Languages of Buddhist Scriptures

Buddhist Scriptures are transmitted in many languages today and this has been the case for as far back as we can see in history.

Buddhist scriptures are the teachings of the Buddha, but they are not in the language that the historical Buddha used, just as the biographies of Jesus give his words in Koiné Greek, even though the historical Jesus spoke another language, like Aramaic.

We do not know what was the actual language that the historical Buddha spoke, although some Buddhist schools--the Theravada, for example--presume that the Buddha spoke in the language in which their own scriptures are preserved. Such statements may not be historically verifiable, but they are important for understanding how some Buddhist communities may think that their scriptures are the literal word of the Buddha, just as he said it.

At the Buddha is remembered in some Buddhist texts as encouraging his disciples to translate his teachings into the languages of the audiences that they were addressing. It does seem, as well, that for the first centuries of Buddhist history, Buddhist scriptures were transmitted orally in a variety of Middle Indic languages. These Middle-Indic languages would have been close to spoken languages of different regions of India at that time. The oldest manuscripts of Buddhist scriptures that we have are in the Gandhari language, which was the language spoken in the region of South Asia that now includes Pakistan and Afghanistan, and these illustrate how Buddhists employed local, vernacular languages for religious purposes.

Later, however, Buddhists began to develop trans-local languages. These were languages that could be understood across linguistic boundaries and sometimes also cultural boundaries. They became languages of learning and also of prestige. Eventually, Buddhist scriptures came to be transmitted exclusively in such 'trans-local,' cosmopolitan languages. The use of such languages is often associated with the spread of Buddhism across Asia and also with its developing social prominence. We might compare these cosmopolitan Buddhist languages with the use of Koiné Greek in early Christianity, the use of Latin in medieval Europe, or the use of Arabic in the Islamic world.

We can say that these trans-local, cosmopolitan languages became the canonical languages of the Buddhist world. This is especially the case with the Pali language, which is the language of the canon of Theravada Buddhism. In fact, however, the word Pali, in Pali, is not the name of name of a language, but is the name of the Theravada canon itself: Pali is thus the name of the language of the canon in which the word of the Buddha is preserved.

In many respects, Pali bears the linguistic features of what we know of various regional or local languages of ancient South Asia. It thus seems like a spoken language that has been written down. In other respects, both in terms of linguistic features and social uses, Pali seems closer to Sanskrit. It is not a spoken language, at all, but rather a new language created by homogenizing various local languages and refined and 'perfected' in the way Sanskrit had been perfected by scholarly refinement; indeed the word 'sanskrit' itself means 'refined' or 'perfected.'

No one ever had Pali as a mother tongue. Instead Pali was a learned language. It was learned as a way of entering a world of education and prestige. Pali, like Sanskrit, always co-existed with other languages, and Buddhists always used local languages, like Burmese, Thai, or Sinhala, wherever they also used Pali. [More Information about Pali can be found [here](http://www.palitext.com/subpages/lan_lite.htm).]

Once Buddhists began to use cosmopolitan languages for religious purposes, they began to adapt other cosmopolitan languages to transmit the teachings of the Buddha in their scriptures. Some Buddhist scriptures, like portions of the Lotus Sutra, are written in what modern scholars sometimes call "Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit." [For more information on Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit, see [here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_Hybrid_Sanskrit).] Other Buddhist scriptures were transmitted in more standard forms of classical Sanskrit.

When Buddhist scriptures were brought to China and Tibet, they were translated into translocal forms of those languages. Buddhist scriptures in written Chinese could be read across China, even though various kinds of spoken Chinese were also in use, and Chinese translations of Buddhist scriptures were also used by Buddhists in Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. In many respects, the language of Buddhist scriptures was closer to a scholarly language than to a popular spoken language. Buddhist scriptures would always have to be translated into local languages, if they were to be understood by most people.

When Buddhist scriptures were brought to Tibet, a new form of Tibetan was created for their translation. It is not a 'natural' form of Tibetan, but a scholarly one and it was created for the purpose of being able to do very precise translations of Indian texts and not for ease of understanding by everyday Tibetan speakers. In this precision, Tibetan translations were very successful and their precision sometimes allows modern scholars to reconstruct lost Sanskrit texts by 'back-translating' them from Tibetan.

This process of Buddhist translation continues today, of course, with the modern translations of Buddhist scriptures in English, French, and Spanish, for example, for the use of Buddhists who speak those languages.

## Reading: Buddhist Scriptures as Dharma

Buddhist scriptures are the word of the Buddha, but that is not all they are. They are Dharma.

We saw something of the significance of approaching Buddhist scriptures as Dharma (or Dhamma, in Pali) in our first class. To call the teaching of the Buddha *Dharma* is to say that they are more than texts.

They are a refuge, as we have seen, in our first class.

Dharma also means Reality, with a capital "R," or Truth, with a capital "T." Dharma, as Reality, is the way things really are, in contrast to whatever ways we perceive the world according to our own delusions and illusions. It is a Reality that we need to awaken to, just as the Buddha, the Awakened One, has. It is a Truth, knowing which sets us free.

Buddhists have disagreed about the specifics of the nature of this Reality and they have disagreed about the contours of this Truth that sets us free, but they have been unanimous in saying that what the Buddha taught--his Dharma--gives us access to insight into Reality--the Dharma--and that when we see things as they are, we are set free from the suffering and turmoil in which we usually live.

These two meanings of Dharma mean that the Buddha's teachings are presented as being connected to "facts" that are important for us to know, as in the following example:

There are these five facts that one should reflect on often, whether one is a woman or a man, lay or ordained. Which five?

"I am subject to aging, have not gone beyond aging"...

"I am subject to illness, have not gone beyond illness"...

"I am subject to death, have not gone beyond death"...

"I will grow different, separate from all that is dear and appealing to me"...

"I am the owner of my actions (kamma), heir to my actions, born of my actions, related through my actions, and have my actions as my arbitrator. Whatever I do, for good or for evil, to that will I fall heir"...

These are the five facts that one should reflect on often, whether one is a woman or a man, lay or ordained.

— [Anguttara Nikaya V.57](http://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an05/an05.057.than.html)

Dharma is also threefold:

1. the Dharma to be studied, as in the texts of Buddhist scriptures and all the texts associated with these scriptures, such as commentaries.

2. the Dharma to be practiced, which is to live how the Buddha taught.

3. the Dharma to be realized, which is to awake, to experience for oneself Reality, to know things as they really are. This Dharma is transcendent and like all experiences always goes far beyond what can be put into words.

Each kind of Dharma leads on to the next. We only know what we are studying when we learn it through practice. Reading about playing the piano is not the same as playing a piano.

Some Buddhist texts warn against missing this: to get stuck on only studying texts and not following them to where they lead:

64. Though all his life a fool associates with a wise man, he no more comprehends the Truth than a spoon tastes the flavor of the soup.

65. Though only for a moment a discerning person associates with a wise man, quickly he comprehends the Truth, just as the tongue tastes the flavor of the soup.

[Dhammapada 64-65](http://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/dhp/dhp.05.budd.html)

Of course, as significant a warning as this metaphor may be, we don't have to be Buddhas to study Buddhist scriptures, we can learn much about then, about Buddhists and about ourselves. Obviously there is much to learn as long as we always engage Buddhist scriptures as we engage anything human: by observing and by inferring.

## Reading: Tipitaka

As we have emphasized so far in this class, there is no one single canon that is accepted by all Buddhists, but looking at one canon, the Pali canon, can give us something of a basic idea of how a canon is a collection of diverse texts, brought together thematically. The basic structure of the Pali canon is important to learn, since it helps us to see some crucial distinctions in Buddhist scriptures.

The word tipitaka (Sanskrit, tripitaka) means "three baskets." The name is a metaphor for what the Pali canon is: a set of three 'containers' in which books of common themes and approaches are put. The three baskets are the Vinaya pitaka, the Sutta pitaka, and the Abhidhamma pitaka.

You can find an overview of the formation of the Pali canon and its organization in this [Introduction](https://books.google.com/books/about/In_the_Buddha_s_Words.html?id=11X1h60Qc0IC&printsec=frontcover&source=kp_read_button#v=onepage&q&f=false) to an anthology of texts from the Pali Canon by Bhikkhu Bodhi; you will want to read pages 5-14.

Knowing the three divisions is what is most important for us at this point and you should click on the links below for each of the three divisions and read what you find there. Other links might be followed to read about sub-divisions within Sutta pitaka, if you want to read more.

[Vinaya Pitaka](http://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/vin/index.html)

The collection of texts concerning the rules of conduct governing the daily affairs within the Sangha — the community of *bhikkhus* (ordained monks) and *bhikkhunis* (ordained nuns). Far more than merely a list of rules, the Vinaya Pitaka also includes the stories behind the origin of each rule, providing a detailed account of the Buddha's solution to the question of how to maintain communal harmony within a large and diverse spiritual community.

[Sutta Pitaka](http://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sutta.html)

The collection of suttas, or discourses, attributed to the Buddha and a few of his closest disciples, containing all the central teachings of Theravada Buddhism. The suttas are divided among five nikayas (collections):

- [Digha Nikaya](http://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/dn/index.html) — the "long collection"
- [Majjhima Nikaya](http://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/index.html) — the "middle-length collection"
- [Samyutta Nikaya](http://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/index.html) — the "grouped collection"
- [Anguttara Nikaya](http://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/index.html) — the "further-factored collection"
- [Khuddaka Nikaya](http://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/index.html) — the "collection of little texts":
  - Khuddakapatha
  - Dhammapada
  - Udana
  - Itivuttaka
  - Sutta Nipata
  - Vimanavatthu
  - Petavatthu
  - Theragatha
  - Therigatha
  - Jataka
  - Niddesa
  - Patisambhidamagga
  - Apadana
  - Buddhavamsa
  - Cariyapitaka

[Abhidhamma Pitaka](http://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/abhi/index.html)

The collection of texts in which the underlying doctrinal principles presented in the Sutta Pitaka are reworked and reorganized into a systematic framework that can be applied to an investigation into the nature of mind and matter.

The teachings found in the Sutta pitaka are sometimes described as the Buddha teaching "with examples" and the Abhidhamma is the Buddha teaching abstractly or theoretically, without concrete examples.

## Reading: Some Academic Distinctions: Open/Closed Canons and Dual Canons

Here are two key distinctions that the academic study of religion gives us which will help us to understand the dynamics of Buddhist scriptures in the rest of this course. Here we will just introduce the concepts, but you should learn them now because we will be making frequent reference to them in the days ahead.

I. A closed versus an open canon: in a "closed" canon, new books cannot be added to the list of texts that are considered to be "canonical," nor can texts be removed. In a closed canon, the canon is complete as it is. In contrast, in an "open" canon, new books can be added, whether by some process of new 'revelation' or through the discovery of texts that had become lost somehow.

The Pali canon, or Tipitaka, which you were just reading about, is a closed canon today, but we can see from a study of its history that it was once an open canon, with new books added to it over the centuries. Indeed, some scholars say that the Vinaya pitaka and the first four sections of the Sutta pitaka were the original Pali canon and all the other parts were added to this "open" Pali canon, including the Abhidhamma, until the Pali canon was finally closed.

Most Buddhist canons, however, are not like the Pali canon. They are, to the contrary, open. This is true of the canons of Buddhist scriptures found in Mahayana Buddhism. These canons are so open that the basic structure of the three baskets no longer seems to make sense.

II. Dual canons. Some religions have "dual canons," in which one body of scripture is accepted but a first canon is generally interpreted on its own terms but also in the light of a second, more valued canon. Christianity is an example of a religion with a dual canon: The Hebrew Scriptures are taken as an Old Testament to be interpreted in the light of the New Testament.

Dual canons are extremely common in the Buddhist world. We could say that the Pali canon, the tipitaka itself is a dual canon, with the Vinaya and Sutta pitakas being the first canon and the Abhidhamma pitaka being the second used to underscore the doctrinal positions of the first canon.

Mayayana canons are almost always dual canons, with Mahayana texts re-interpreting what was said in texts found in canons like the Pali canon. We will see this again and again in the classes ahead.

## Reading: Scriptures like Water: The Mountains and Rivers Sutra

If Dharma as something to be studied teaches us about Dharma as Reality, so Dharma as Reality teaches us something about Dharma to be studied.

We can see this in the following statement by Dogen, a medieval Japanese Zen Master. His "Mountains and Rivers Sutra" looks at the natural world as teaching us, just as Buddhist scriptures do. Dharma to be studied and Dharma as Reality are connected to each other, they interpenetrate each other.

Look at this passage with this in mind, ask yourself what do we learn about a Buddhist scripture, as Dharma to be studied, from observing water:

"Descending to earth, it becomes rivers and streams." We should realize that, when water descends to earth, it becomes rivers and streams, and that the essence of rivers and streams becomes sages. The foolish common folk think that water is always in rivers, streams, and seas, but this is not so: [water] makes rivers and seas within water. Therefore, water is in places that are not rivers and seas; it is just that, when water descends to earth, it works as rivers and seas.

Moreover, we should not study that, when water has become rivers and seas, there is then no world and no buddha land [within water]: incalculable buddha lands are realized even within a single drop of water. Consequently, it is not that water exists within the buddha land, nor that the buddha land exists within water: the existence of water has nothing whatever to do with the three times or the dharma realm. Nevertheless, though it is like this, it is the koan of the actualization of water.

Wherever the buddhas and ancestors are, water is always there; wherever water is, there the buddhas and ancestors always appear. Therefore, the buddhas and ancestors have always taken up water as their own body and mind, their own thinking.

However, when humans look at water, they have the one way that sees it only as flowing without rest. This "flow" takes many forms, of which the human view is but one. [Water] flows over the earth; it flows across the sky; it flows up; it flows down. [Water] flows around bends and into deep abysses. It mounts up to form clouds; it descends to form pools.

If you like, you can [read all of Dogen's Mountains and Rivers Sutra here](http://global.sotozen-net.or.jp/eng/dharma/pdf/hogen09/hogen09_07.htm).

## Reading: Mahakasyapa - The Flower Sermon

The short story below comes from a Zen collection of meditation topics called the Mumonkan. It tells about how the Buddha preached a sermon by holding up a flower and how Kasho--or Mahakashyapa, who is so important in the accounts of the First Council that we looked at earlier--understood what the Buddha was teaching. In many ways, the story is distinctively and stereotypically Zen. Zen maintains that it directly transmits the Buddha's religious experiences and the life of Zen lies in the fact of this transmission from a Master to his disciple.

Long ago when the World-Honored One was at Mount Grdhrakuta to give a talk, he held up a flower before the assemblage. At this all remained silent. The Venerable Kasho alone broke into a smile. The World-Honored One said, "I have the all-pervading True Dharma, incomparable Nirvana, exquisite teaching of formless form. It does not rely on letters and is transmitted outside scriptures. I now hand it to Maha Kasho.

[Zenkei Shibayama, Zen Comments on the Mumonkan (New York: Harper and Row, 1974), 58]

This story is very short, but note that it has two components. First is a sermon given in the form of flower, second is the Buddha's reaction to Mahakasyapa's smile, which is about transmission and the authenticity of what was passed on.
